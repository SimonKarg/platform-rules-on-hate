Platform policies · Counterspeech in the rules

How platforms treat counterspeech

Counterspeech means answering hateful or harmful content with speech of your own: condemning it, correcting it, mocking it. We searched every sentence of nine platforms' Community Guidelines, 2022 to October 2026, for what they say about it.

What the rules say today

Which of the nine platforms' Community Guidelines, as of October 2026, do each of these things. The small number is how many provisions do it (a list item with its sub-items, or a sentence in running text); Reporting is shown for comparison: it is the other way the rules ask users to act.

Six things that stand out

X · encouraged

Only X says it promotes counterspeech, yet it cut two concrete protections

“This approach allows many forms of speech to exist on our platform and, in particular, promotes counterspeech: speech that presents facts to correct misstatements or misperceptions, points out hypocrisy or contradictions, warns of offline or online consequences, denounces hateful or dangerous speech, or helps change minds and disarm.”X, Our approach to policy development and enforcement philosophy. In place at first capture, Oct 2022, and still there.

The principle stayed. Two concrete protections did not. Until early 2023, sharing an attacker's manifesto was banned only “with no additional context or counterspeech”. Since then X says it “may also remove Posts disseminating manifestos”, with no exception for counterspeech. And “Counterspeech. We allow for direct responses to misleading information…” went when X folded its manipulated-media policy into its authenticity policy in July 2025.

X, Meta · community notes

Crowd-sourced correction arrived, but only in the misinformation rules

“When you select ‘Civic Integrity’ in the reporting flow, we will route your submission to request a Community Note, a collaborative way to add helpful context to posts.”X, Civic integrity policy, since Nov 2025

X added Community Notes to its enforcement options in April 2023. Meta followed in March 2025 (“Beginning in the United States, the Community Notes program lets people add more context…”) and since April 2025 describes its fact-checking partners as working “outside of the United States”. Neither mentions notes in its hate or harassment rules. TikTok's rules still lean on “independent fact-checkers” and do not mention its Footnotes feature.

TikTok · exempt, with limits

TikTok draws the line other platforms leave implicit: no retaliation

“Criticizing harassment or bullying behavior, as long as it does not involve retaliatory harassment”TikTok, Safety and civility: Harassment and bullying, allowed. Since Sep 2025

From October 2023 to September 2025 the same rule said “counter speech” outright (“Counterspeech, or condemnation of harassment or bullying…”). The September 2025 rewrite swapped the word for “criticism” in both the hate and the harassment rules. “Counterspeech” survives as one of six public interest exceptions on TikTok's enforcement page.

Meta · exempt

Meta's exceptions multiplied, but outside its hate rule

“We recognize that people sometimes share content that includes slurs or someone else’s speech in order to condemn the speech or report on it.”Meta, Hateful conduct policy. In 2022 and 2023 the purpose was “to condemn it or raise awareness”; “report on it” arrived in February 2024.

Exceptions for content shared to condemn or raise awareness now appear across Meta's standards: violence, fraud, sexual exploitation, dangerous organizations, coordinating harm. The hate rule itself holds one such sentence.

LinkedIn · exempt

LinkedIn labels counterspeech instead of removing it, and lets members call people out

“We also allow members to call out the harassing, hateful, or otherwise abusive conduct of others, so long as the focus remains on driving objective awareness to the behavior, rather than personally attacking or inciting harassment against those individuals.”LinkedIn, Harassment or abusive content. In place at first capture, Nov 2022

Its hate rule adds: “We may label rather than remove content that evokes hateful rhetoric (including slurs) in the context of counter speech, reclamation, or members’ personal experiences…” LinkedIn and X are the only platforms whose rules use the word in a sentence today. TikTok uses it as a one-word list item.

Snapchat, Truth Social · silent

Two rulebooks say nothing about counterspeech; all of them ask users to report

“TRUTH Social provides a variety of features and functions -- including the ability to mute or block other users, or to mute or block all comments containing terms of your choice -- and we encourage you to use these tools whenever you wish to control your content.”Truth Social, Community Guidelines, first captured Mar 2024

Snapchat's and Truth Social's rules have no carve-out for counterspeech, and no sentence that encourages or limits it. Snapchat points users to reporting and blocking; Truth Social to muting and blocking.

How this was counted

Exempt
A carve-out from a rule: content that would otherwise break it (a slur, a manifesto, a graphic video) is kept up, or labelled instead of removed, when it is shared to condemn, criticise, counter, refute, mock or raise awareness of the harm. Includes the sentences that set up such a carve-out. Split by wording into condemn or counter (condemn, criticise, counter, refute, mock, satirise, respond, support victims) and raise awareness (draw attention, inform, educate, document, warn); a provision can be both. Not counted: general room for satire, humour, news or debate, criticism of public figures and policies, reclaimed slurs.
Encouraged
The platform says it values, promotes or asks for counterspeech or speaking up.
Community notes
Features through which users add context to other people's posts.
Limits
Rules that bound how users may respond: retaliation, call-outs that become harassment, pile-ons and brigading, misuse of reporting or of moderation tools, and cases where a good motive does not excuse sharing (“even if shared to raise awareness”).
Tone
The platform asks users to engage with civility, respect, empathy or constructively, describes the supportive climate it wants, or points users to supporting others.
Report
For comparison: sentences that tell users they can or should report content. Found by rule (wording patterns), not by hand.
